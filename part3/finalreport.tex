\section{Accessibility Report}
\label{secaccessibilityreport}

\subsection{Rationale and Requirements Traceability}
\label{subseca11yrationale}

Accessibility was treated as a condition for completing the core journey, not
as a visual enhancement. Part 1 defined an international student persona who
must judge unfamiliar listings and people with limited local knowledge and
English, and a domestic shared-living persona who depends on household
agreements staying visible after move-in. Both rely on interface information
that survives a change of device, language or input method. The test group
reflected this: a participant with colour vision deficiency, a mobile-first
international student and a participant whose first language was not English.
If status depends on colour alone, dialogs lose keyboard focus or translated
rules are read with English pronunciation, these users cannot judge trust
information, message safely or understand a household agreement.

Two requirements set the baseline. NFR-03 requires every Sprint 1 screen
(S-01 to S-06) to support keyboard use and meet WCAG 2.2 AA contrast
thresholds, and NFR-06 requires the household rules view to serve at least
five languages. The assessment brief, however, requests the WCAG 2.0
checklist. Therefore, Table~\ref{taba11ychecklist} reports WCAG 2.0 success
criteria with their conformance level while retaining WCAG 2.2 AA as the
broader target. Accessibility was traced to the functional work it enabled:
US-11 with AC-US11-01 and AC-US11-02 for recognising verified profiles; US-09
and AC-US09-04 for understanding a blocked contact-detail message; US-02 and
AC-US02-02 for translated household rules; and US-10 with AC-US10-02 and
AC-US10-04 for proposing a rule and interpreting approval and activation
states.

\subsection{Evaluation Scope and Method}
\label{subseca11ymethod}

The report structure adapts the W3C accessibility evaluation report
template:\footnote{W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, \textit{Accessibility
Evaluation Report Template}, \url{https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/report-template/}.}
scope and methods, findings, recommended actions and limitations. The success
criteria and conformance levels follow the WCAG 2.0 checklist named in the
assessment brief, supplemented by the A11Y Project checklist for plain-language
phrasing.\footnote{W3C, \textit{WCAG 2.0 Checklist (Working Draft, Appendix B)},
\url{https://www.w3.org/TR/2006/WD-WCAG20-20060427/appendixB.html}; The A11Y
Project, \textit{Checklist}, \url{https://www.a11yproject.com/checklist/}.}

The scope covered the six Sprint 1 screens (S-01 to S-06) of the hosted
prototype together with the login and notification dialogs that gate or
interrupt those tasks.\footnote{Evaluated build:
\url{http://roommate-haven-dev.eba-2h6p4mxq.ap-southeast-2.elasticbeanstalk.com}.}
The Create roster dialog is outside the Sprint 1 release scope defined in
Part 2, but it reuses the dialog and form-validation components that S-05 and
S-06 depend on, so it was sampled as a component-level proxy and is labelled as
such below. On 27 July 2026, a Playwright-assisted review of keyboard
behaviour, focus, DOM and ARIA state, language metadata, contrast and
responsive layouts at 1470 by 726 and 390 by 844 pixels identified nine
accessibility findings. Screenshots recorded visible states; focus and
announcement claims came from interaction and DOM assertions.

Between 25 and 27 July, five participants completed moderated tasks T1 to T5
using tablet, laptop and mobile devices. Think-aloud comments, errors, assistance and task effects
were recorded. This added human evidence: User A lost context when focus escaped
a dialog, and User B could not operate Discover tabs with arrow keys and found
their colour-dependent selected state unclear. After Sprint 2 changes, the team
ran Node tests (7/7 passed) and Playwright regression checks at 1440 by 1000 and
390 by 844 pixels. A criterion was marked \textit{Met} only where the revised
prototype and regression evidence addressed the WCAG requirement; unresolved
or incompletely sampled requirements were marked \textit{Partially met}.

\subsection{Limitations and Next Steps}
\label{subseca11ylimitations}

This evaluation does not establish full conformance. Results marked
\textit{Met} are met on the components sampled, not proven across every
screen. No participant used a screen reader, switch device or voice control,
and seeded session data may hide barriers that production authentication,
asynchronous errors and dynamic updates will introduce. The team will next run
NVDA with Chrome and VoiceOver with Safari, add automated axe checks to
Playwright, and repeat the keyboard walkthrough for S-01 to S-06. The
remaining interface work is to label the desktop contact-sharing icon, combine
approval count and activation condition into one announced status, translate
the full household-rule workflow, and apply the roster error pattern to all
Sprint 1 forms. Screen-reader and low-vision participants will then validate
these changes in practice.

\section{Testing Report}
\label{sectestingreport}

\subsection{Testing Rationale and Objectives}
\label{subsectestingrationale}

The usability test assessed whether the prototype allowed participants to
complete the core tasks without help. It checked each acceptance criterion and
showed where users misunderstood a control, a message, or a change in system
state. The team compared observed behaviour with completion data and participant
comments. The findings were then used to decide which requirements and screens
needed revision before the next iteration.

\subsection{Participants and Method}
\label{subsectestingmethod}

\subsubsection{Participants}

Five purposively selected participants covered student and working-adult
contexts, different rental and shared-living experience, colour-accessibility,
mobile-first use and different English-language backgrounds
(Table~\ref{tabiterationoneparticipants}). They were recruited through the
project team network and screened for these contexts. Participation was
voluntary and unpaid, and consent was obtained before each session.

Each moderated session used the same seeded account and task state.

were available, so individual time-threshold compliance could not be confirmed
and will be captured in Iteration 2.

\subsection{Iteration 1 Testing}
\label{subseciterationone}

\subsubsection{Test Plan and Acceptance Criteria}

Iteration 1 retained the five tasks and acceptance-criteria identifiers from
Part 2, but expanded coverage from the original mobile viewport to tablet,
laptop and mobile contexts. Task outcome and acceptance-criteria status were
assessed separately. A task was \textit{Completed} only when at least four of
five participants independently met every behaviour, interpretation and time
condition in its measurable threshold. It was \textit{Partially completed}
when participants reached some or all of the workflow but one or more
conditions were failed or not separately tested. Each acceptance criterion was
classified as Pass, Partial, Fail or Not separately tested. Partial means that
some required behaviour was demonstrated but the full measurable threshold was
not met; task completion alone did not override a criterion-level result.
AC-US11-01 and AC-US09-01 used seeded, simulated states, so testing assessed
usability rather than production verification or messaging security.

\subsection{Persistent Issues and Impact}
\label{subsecpersistentissues}

The same five participants tested both iterations. Several still struggled to
explain what the compatibility score measured after the second test. Although
the revised cards linked the score to saved preferences, some users treated a
high score as evidence that a person was safe. This could cause them to
misjudge a potential housemate.

Confusion also persisted between a verified profile and phone verification.
Similar visual cues made the two states appear equivalent, even though identity
verification supports profile trust and phone verification only unlocks
messaging. The ambiguity increased decision time and weakened confidence in
the safety process.

Navigation remained unclear because the main entry was named Household while
the destination was named House rules. Users opened the wrong view before
finding rule proposals. They also struggled in both rounds to connect approval
progress with activation. These problems caused repeated checking and left
users uncertain about which household rules applied.
