\section{Accessibility Report}
\label{secaccessibilityreport}

\subsection{Rationale and Requirements Traceability}
\label{subseca11yrationale}

Accessibility was considered an essential part of the user experience rather than
just a visual feature. In interim report, we identified an international student persona
who needs to evaluate unfamiliar accommodation listings and potential housemates
despite having limited local knowledge and English proficiency. We also identified
a domestic shared-living persona who relies on household agreements remaining
clear and accessible after moving in. Both users depend on information that can
be understood across different devices, languages, and interaction methods.

This was also reflected in our user testing. Participants included a user with
colour vision deficiency, a mobile-first international student, and a participant
whose first language was not English. Their feedback showed that if important
information relies only on colour, keyboard navigation is interrupted, or
translated content is difficult to understand, users may struggle to judge the
trustworthiness of listings, communicate safely, or understand household
agreements.

Two non-functional requirements formed the basis of the accessibility evaluation.
NFR-03 requires all Sprint 1 screens (S-01 to S-06) to support keyboard
navigation and meet WCAG 2.2 AA colour contrast requirements, while NFR-06
requires the household rules page to support at least five languages. However,
the assessment brief recommends evaluating the prototype using the WCAG 2.0
checklist. Therefore, Table~\ref{taba11ychecklist} reports the prototype against
the relevant WCAG 2.0 success criteria while using WCAG 2.2 AA as the overall
design target.

Accessibility requirements were also linked directly to the project's user
stories and acceptance criteria. US-11 (AC-US11-01 and AC-US11-02) supports
recognition of verified profiles, US-09 (AC-US09-04) ensures users can
understand blocked contact-detail messages, US-02 (AC-US02-02) focuses on
translated household rules, and US-10 (AC-US10-02 and AC-US10-04) supports
creating, approving, and activating household rules. Linking accessibility to
these user stories ensured that accessibility requirements were considered
throughout the design process instead of being added at the end.

\subsection{Evaluation Scope and Method}
\label{subseca11ymethod}

This report follows the structure of the W3C Accessibility Evaluation Report
Template,\footnote{W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, \textit{Accessibility
Evaluation Report Template}, \url{https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/report-template/}.}
including the evaluation scope, methods, findings, recommended actions, and
limitations. Accessibility was evaluated using the WCAG 2.0 success criteria
specified in the assessment brief, with the A11Y Project checklist used as an
additional reference to support plain-language interpretation of the
guidelines.\footnote{W3C, \textit{WCAG 2.0 Checklist (Working Draft, Appendix B)},
\url{https://www.w3.org/TR/2006/WD-WCAG20-20060427/appendixB.html}; The A11Y
Project, \textit{Checklist}, \url{https://www.a11yproject.com/checklist/}.}

The evaluation covered the six Sprint 1 prototype screens (S-01 to S-06),
together with the login and notification dialogs that support these user
tasks.\footnote{Evaluated build:
\url{http://roommate-haven-dev.eba-2h6p4mxq.ap-southeast-2.elasticbeanstalk.com}.}
Although the Create Roster dialog was outside the Sprint 1 release scope, it
reused the same dialog and form-validation components as Screens S-05 and
S-06. It was therefore included as a component-level sample to assess the
accessibility of shared interface elements.

The prototype was reviewed using Playwright-assisted testing to examine
keyboard navigation, focus management, DOM and ARIA attributes, language
metadata, colour contrast, and responsive layouts at resolutions of
1470 × 726 and 390 × 844 pixels. Nine accessibility issues were identified.
Screenshots were captured to document the interface state, while focus
behaviour and screen-reader related observations were verified through user
interaction and DOM inspection.

Five participants completed moderated usability tasks (T1–T5) on laptop,
tablet, and mobile devices. Think-aloud comments, task completion, user
errors, requests for assistance, and task outcomes were recorded throughout
the sessions. These observations provided additional evidence beyond
automated testing. For example, one participant lost context when keyboard
focus escaped from a dialog, while another participant found the Discover
tabs difficult to navigate using the keyboard and reported that the selected
tab relied too heavily on colour alone.

Following the Sprint 2 improvements, the team repeated automated Node testing
(7 of 7 tests passed) together with Playwright regression testing at
1440 × 1000 and 390 × 844 pixels. A WCAG criterion was recorded as
\textit{Met} only when both the updated prototype and the supporting
regression evidence demonstrated compliance. Criteria that could not yet be
fully verified or required implementation beyond the prototype were recorded
as \textit{Partially met}.

\subsection{Limitations and Next Steps}
\label{subseca11ylimitations}

This evaluation does not establish full conformance. Results marked
\textit{Met} are met on the components sampled, not proven across every
screen. No participant used a screen reader, switch device or voice control,
and seeded session data may hide barriers that production authentication,
asynchronous errors and dynamic updates will introduce. The team will next run
NVDA with Chrome and VoiceOver with Safari, add automated axe checks to
Playwright, and repeat the keyboard walkthrough for S-01 to S-06. The
remaining interface work is to label the desktop contact-sharing icon, combine
approval count and activation condition into one announced status, translate
the full household-rule workflow, and apply the roster error pattern to all
Sprint 1 forms. Screen-reader and low-vision participants will then validate
these changes in practice.

\section{Testing Report}
\label{sectestingreport}

\subsection{Testing Rationale and Objectives}
\label{subsectestingrationale}

The usability test assessed whether the prototype allowed participants to
complete the core tasks without help. It checked each acceptance criterion and
showed where users misunderstood a control, a message, or a change in system
state. The team compared observed behaviour with completion data and participant
comments. The findings were then used to decide which requirements and screens
needed revision before the next iteration.

\subsection{Participants and Method}
\label{subsectestingmethod}

\subsubsection{Participants}

contexts, different rental and shared-living experience, colour-accessibility,
mobile-first use and different English-language backgrounds
(Table~\ref{tabiterationoneparticipants}). They were recruited through the
project team network and screened for these contexts. Participation was
voluntary and unpaid, and consent was obtained before each session.

Each moderated session used the same seeded account and task state.
Participants attempted T1 to T5 while thinking aloud. The facilitator gave a

counted as errors; assisted completions were not independent. Only task means
were available, so individual time-threshold compliance could not be confirmed
and will be captured in Iteration 2.

\subsection{Iteration 1 Testing}
\label{subseciterationone}

\subsubsection{Test Plan and Acceptance Criteria}

Iteration 1 retained the five tasks and acceptance-criteria identifiers from
Part 2, but expanded coverage from the original mobile viewport to tablet,
laptop and mobile contexts. Task outcome and acceptance-criteria status were
assessed separately. A task was \textit{Completed} only when at least four of
five participants independently met every behaviour, interpretation and time
condition in its measurable threshold. It was \textit{Partially completed}
when participants reached some or all of the workflow but one or more
conditions were failed or not separately tested. Each acceptance criterion was
classified as Pass, Partial, Fail or Not separately tested. Partial means that
some required behaviour was demonstrated but the full measurable threshold was
not met; task completion alone did not override a criterion-level result.
AC-US11-01 and AC-US09-01 used seeded, simulated states, so testing assessed
usability rather than production verification or messaging security.

\subsection{Persistent Issues and Impact}
\label{subsecpersistentissues}

The same five participants tested both iterations. Several still struggled to
explain what the compatibility score measured after the second test. Although
the revised cards linked the score to saved preferences, some users treated a
high score as evidence that a person was safe. This could cause them to
misjudge a potential housemate.

Confusion also persisted between a verified profile and phone verification.
Similar visual cues made the two states appear equivalent, even though identity
verification supports profile trust and phone verification only unlocks
messaging. The ambiguity increased decision time and weakened confidence in
the safety process.

Navigation remained unclear because the main entry was named Household while
the destination was named House rules. Users opened the wrong view before
finding rule proposals. They also struggled in both rounds to connect approval
progress with activation. These problems caused repeated checking and left
users uncertain about which household rules applied.

\subsection{Evidence Boundaries}
\label{subsecfinalprototypelimitations}

The matrices describe implemented prototype evidence, not unqualified success
across the whole product. The second iteration passed the targeted T1 and T4
criteria, while the compatibility-score interpretation remained partial and
the rule-activation explanation remained failed. Testing also retained the
Household/House rules label mismatch, confusion between identity and phone
verification, an icon-only desktop sharing action and mixed-language controls.
These open findings are reported in Section~\ref{subsecpersistentissues} and
are not counted as completed principles or accessibility requirements above.
